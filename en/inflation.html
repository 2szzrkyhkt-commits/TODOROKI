Inflation, Cash Savings and Investing

Inflation reduces what a fixed amount of money can buy over time. Understanding purchasing power helps put savings and investment risk in context.

01. Inflation and bank deposits

A bank balance can remain unchanged while prices rise. Interest earned on deposits may offset some inflation, but what matters is the after-tax interest rate compared with price increases.

02. How purchasing power changes

At a hypothetical annual inflation rate of 3%, goods costing 1,000,000 yen today would cost about 1,343,916 yen after ten years. Conversely, 1,000,000 yen held as cash would buy roughly what 744,094 yen buys today, assuming the same price growth.

03. Inflation versus deflation

During inflation, holding only non-interest-bearing cash exposes purchasing power to erosion. During deflation, cash may gain purchasing power, but debt burdens can become harder to service. Neither environment makes any asset risk-free.

04. Can stocks protect against inflation?

Companies can sometimes raise selling prices, but higher labor, energy and financing costs can squeeze margins. Stock prices can fall even during inflation. Diversification and valuation remain important.

05. Inflation and wealth differences

Inflation affects households differently depending on wages, debt, savings and ownership of productive assets. Investment returns are uncertain, and market risk should not be ignored.

06. Key takeaway

Compare expected returns after fees and taxes with inflation. Keep money needed in the near term accessible and avoid assuming stocks always outperform inflation.

Inflation, wages and emergency savings

Inflation does not affect every household equally. A person whose wages rise by 2% while their living costs rise by 4% experiences a decline in real purchasing power, even though nominal income increases.

Keeping an emergency cash reserve can reduce the need to sell investments during a downturn. Its purpose is liquidity and resilience, not necessarily beating inflation.

Stocks and real assets may offer long-run exposure to economic growth or pricing power, but their market prices can fall sharply. There is no asset that perfectly protects against every inflation scenario.
