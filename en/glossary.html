Economics & Finance

Deductive reasoning

Deriving a conclusion logically from premises. If the premises are true and the argument is valid, the conclusion must be true.

Example: All overdue cases must be reported; case A is overdue; therefore A must be reported. Check the truth of the premises separately from logical validity.

Economics & Finance

Inductive reasoning

Inferring broader patterns or rules from individual observations. The conclusion is not guaranteed to be true.

Example: Productive mornings suggest that morning work suits you. Check sample size, bias, counterexamples and changing conditions; revise with new evidence.

Economics & Finance

Behavioral finance

The study of how investor cognition, emotions and behavior relate to trading and market prices, including limits to arbitrage.

Example: Investigating the tendency to postpone realizing losses while taking gains early. Examine your own biases without attributing every market movement to psychology.

Economics & Finance

Neuroeconomics

A field combining economics, psychology and neuroscience to study valuation, learning and choice.

Example: Measuring brain activity during decisions involving rewards or risk. Brain activity alone cannot establish personal intentions or predict stock prices.

Related PDF (Japanese) →
Economics & Finance

Experimental economics

Testing economic behavior, theoretical predictions and institutions through experiments with specified incentives, information and trading rules.

Example: Comparing price formation under different auction formats. Study when predictions hold as well as when they fail; generalize cautiously.

Related PDF (Japanese) →
Economics & Finance

Dictator game

An experimental task in which one participant allocates resources and the recipient has no veto.

Example: Deciding how much of ¥1,000 to give another person. It studies distribution preferences and altruism; anonymity and experimental conditions affect results.

Related PDF (Japanese) →
Economics & Finance

Ultimatum game

One participant proposes a split; acceptance implements it, while rejection leaves both participants with zero.

Example: Proposing a split of ¥1,000 for another person to accept or reject. Responses to fairness depend on the population and experimental conditions.

Related PDF (Japanese) →
Economics & Finance

Prisoner’s dilemma

A game in which individually advantageous choices can leave both players worse off than mutual cooperation.

In the standard one-shot game, defection dominates when only individual monetary payoffs count. Repetition, relationships and social preferences can change cooperation; defection is not rational in every situation.

Investing Basics

Regret aversion

Avoiding anticipated regret can influence a decision.

Example: Being unable to sell because you imagine the price rising afterward. Record the evidence available when deciding, rather than judging only by the outcome of an alternative.

Related PDF (Japanese) →
Investing Basics

Herd behavior (herding)

Following others’ actions rather than your own information or judgment.

Example: Buying because a stock is popular on social media, then selling with the crowd in a decline. Others’ actions may contain information, but popularity alone is insufficient.

Related PDF (Japanese) →
Investing Basics

Status quo bias

A tendency to retain the current situation or default even when changing may offer benefits.

Example: Keeping an investment after its original rationale fails. Compare holding, selling and alternatives, including costs and taxes.

Related PDF (Japanese) →
Investing Basics

FOMO (fear of missing out)

Anxiety about missing opportunities or gains enjoyed by others.

Example: Chasing a surging stock without checking the evidence. FOMO may relate to regret aversion, but the terms are not synonymous.

Behavioral Economics

Heuristic

A way of simplifying complex judgments using experience or salient cues. Useful under limited time or information, but sometimes produces systematic bias.

Example: Using a high price as a cue for quality. Price alone does not establish quality.

Related PDF (Japanese) →
Behavioral Economics

Cognitive bias

A systematic tendency in information processing or judgment, distinct from a random error. Motivation and emotion, as well as heuristics, can contribute.

Example: Giving extra weight to favorable information about a stock you want to buy.

Related PDF (Japanese) →
Behavioral Economics

Behavioral economics

The study of actual economic decisions using psychological findings, experiments and observations, including cognitive limits, emotions, self-control and concern for others.

Example: Testing whether presenting the same amount as a gain or a loss changes choices.

Behavioral Economics

Representativeness heuristic

Judging probability or group membership by similarity to a typical image or example, potentially neglecting base rates and small samples.

Example: Expecting a company to keep growing merely because it resembles a past high-growth business.

Related PDF (Japanese) →
Behavioral Economics

Anchoring

An initial number or reference can exert excessive influence on later estimates and judgments.

Example: Treating a ¥600 stock as cheap because it previously traded at ¥1,000. Also examine current earnings, assets and prospects.

Related PDF (Japanese) →
Behavioral Economics

Confirmation bias

A tendency to seek or emphasize evidence supporting your beliefs and discount opposing evidence.

Example: Reading only bullish commentary while overlooking weak earnings. Write down disconfirming evidence and conditions for changing your view in advance.

Related PDF (Japanese) →
Behavioral Economics

Availability heuristic

Judging frequency or probability by how easily examples come to mind or can be imagined.

Example: Overestimating investment success after repeatedly seeing success stories online. Check the full sample and failures too.

Related PDF (Japanese) →
Behavioral Economics

Overconfidence bias

Overestimating your knowledge, forecast accuracy or ability relative to others.

Example: Increasing investments or borrowing after a few successes. Record forecasts and outcomes, and manage maximum losses.

Science & Technology

Bayesian learning

Updating the probability of a hypothesis as new evidence arrives: posterior probability is proportional to likelihood multiplied by prior probability.

Example: Update hypotheses about the proportion of red balls in a bag after each draw. The posterior becomes the next prior. Results depend on priors, model assumptions and data quality; do not count the same evidence twice.

Economics & Finance

Nash equilibrium

A combination of strategies in which no participant can improve their payoff by changing only their own strategy, given the others’ strategies. Each is a best response to the others.

Example: Both firms earn 3 at high prices and 1 at low prices; if only one cuts its price, it earns 4 and the other 0. Both choosing low prices is an equilibrium. Equilibria need not be collectively best and may be multiple; information, rules and repetition matter.

Economics & Finance

Homo economicus (economic agent)

A simplified economic model of a person who acts rationally to maximize their own benefit or utility, sometimes described as extremely rational, self-controlled and self-interested.

Example: Comparing price, quality and future benefits without yielding to immediate temptation. Real people exhibit biases, limited self-control and concern for others; behavioral economics studies these differences.

Economics & Finance

Prospect theory

A model of choices under risk based on gains and losses relative to a reference point, loss aversion, diminishing sensitivity and probability weighting. Risk preferences depend on the situation.

Behavioral Economics

Loss aversion

The tendency to weigh losses more heavily than comparable gains. Its strength varies; losses are not universally felt at twice the intensity of gains.

Economics & Finance

Reference dependence

Evaluating outcomes relative to a benchmark such as a purchase price or expectation. This differs from anchoring on an initially presented number.

Investing Basics

Disposition effect

The tendency to sell winning assets and retain losing assets. Taxes, liquidity needs and expectations can also influence sales.

Economics & Finance

Transaction utility

Satisfaction or dissatisfaction from comparing the price paid with a reference price. Feeling that a share is a bargain does not establish fundamental undervaluation.

Related PDF (Japanese) →
Economics & Finance

Acquisition utility

Utility from the benefit acquired relative to the payment required, distinct from satisfaction with a deal relative to a reference price.

Related PDF (Japanese) →
Economics & Finance

Endowment effect

The tendency to value something more because one owns it. This differs from satisfaction with buying at a low price.

Related PDF (Japanese) →
Economics & Finance

Nudge

A change in choice architecture that predictably influences behaviour without forbidding options or materially changing economic incentives, and is easy and inexpensive to avoid. A binding trading limit is a constraint instead.

Science & Technology

Processor

A device that performs calculations and controls equipment. CPUs are a common example; MPU usually means microprocessor. GPUs are also processors.

Investing Basics

TSE Prime Market

The Tokyo Stock Exchange segment for companies with scale, liquidity and higher corporate governance standards suitable for many institutional investors.

Usage & cautions: Prime, Standard and Growth are market segments, not rankings of quality or cheapness.

Investing Basics

TSE Standard Market

The Tokyo Stock Exchange segment for companies with a certain level of liquidity and the basic governance standards expected of listed companies.

Usage & cautions: Market segments do not rank companies by quality or valuation.

Investing Basics

TSE Growth Market

The Tokyo Stock Exchange segment for companies with high growth potential. Limited operating track records can entail relatively high risk.

Usage & cautions: The segment does not guarantee growth or indicate whether a share is cheap.

Investing Basics

Screening

Narrowing a list of securities using specified criteria.

Usage & cautions: A way to find candidates, not a substitute for company analysis.

Valuation & Ratios

P/E ratio (PER)

Share price relative to earnings per common share.

Formula / calculation basis
Share price ÷ EPS

Example: ¥1,500 ÷ ¥100 = 15×.

Usage & cautions: Compare peers and historical levels. Usually not meaningful for loss-making companies.

Related articlesPrice and value →
Valuation & Ratios

Earnings per share (EPS)

Profit attributable to each common share.

Formula / calculation basis
Net income attributable to common shareholders ÷ weighted-average common shares

Example: ¥1 billion ÷ 10 million shares = ¥100.

Usage & cautions: Used in P/E and earnings growth analysis. Adjust for stock splits.

Valuation & Ratios

Earnings yield

Earnings as a percentage of the share price; the reciprocal of a positive P/E.

Formula / calculation basis
EPS ÷ share price × 100 = 100 ÷ positive P/E

Example: A P/E of 20× implies 5%.

Usage & cautions: A comparison input for bond yields and the cost of capital.

Related articlesPrice and value →
Valuation & Ratios

Shiller P/E (CAPE)

Valuation using average inflation-adjusted earnings over ten years.

Formula / calculation basis
Real index level ÷ average real EPS over ten years

Example: Smooths earnings fluctuations over the business cycle.

Usage & cautions: For long-term market valuation, not short-term timing. Labels such as below 10×, 10–20× and above 20× are not universal thresholds; country, period and interest rates matter.

Valuation & Ratios

P/B ratio (PBR)

Share price relative to book value per common share.

Formula / calculation basis
Share price ÷ BPS

Example: ¥1,200 ÷ ¥1,000 = 1.2×.

Usage & cautions: Check asset quality and ROE. Below 1× does not by itself prove undervaluation.

Related articlesPrice and value →
Valuation & Ratios

Book value per share (BPS)

Net assets attributable to each common share.

Formula / calculation basis
Equity attributable to common shareholders ÷ common shares at period-end

Example: ¥10 billion ÷ 10 million shares = ¥1,000.

Usage & cautions: Used in P/B. Check goodwill and asset valuations.

Valuation & Ratios

Graham P/E × P/B product

A combined valuation measure multiplying P/E by P/B.

Formula / calculation basis
P/E × P/B

Example: 15 × 1.5 = 22.5.

Usage & cautions: Graham's traditional guideline of 22.5 is not a universal rule.

Valuation & Ratios

Return on equity (ROE)

Profitability relative to shareholders' equity.

Formula / calculation basis
Net income ÷ average equity × 100

Example: ¥2 billion ÷ ¥10 billion × 100 = 20%.

Usage & cautions: Leverage and a smaller equity base can raise ROE. Assess alongside ROA.

Valuation & Ratios

Return on assets (ROA)

Profitability relative to the company's total assets.

Formula / calculation basis
Net income ÷ average total assets × 100

Example: ¥1 billion ÷ ¥20 billion × 100 = 5%.

Usage & cautions: Compare with industry asset structures in mind.

Valuation & Ratios

EV/EBITDA

Enterprise value relative to EBITDA.

Formula / calculation basis
EV ÷ EBITDA; EV ≈ market capitalization + interest-bearing debt − cash and cash equivalents

Example: ¥30 billion ÷ ¥3 billion = 10×.

Usage & cautions: Consider capital expenditure and lease liabilities separately.

Valuation & Ratios

Price-to-cash-flow ratio (PCFR)

Share price relative to cash flow per share.

Formula / calculation basis
Share price ÷ cash flow per share

Example: ¥2,000 ÷ ¥200 = 10×.

Usage & cautions: Check whether cash flow means operating cash flow or an alternative approximation.

Finance & Accounting

Operating cash flow

Cash generated or used by core operating activities.

Formula / calculation basis
Operating activities section of the cash flow statement

Example: Positive operating cash flow still needs to be assessed against capital expenditure.

Usage & cautions: Compare profit with cash generation and examine long-term stability.

Finance & Accounting

Investing cash flow

Cash movements from investments. Purchases of equipment or securities usually reduce cash; disposals usually increase it.

Formula / calculation basis
Investing activities section of the cash flow statement

Example: ¥3 billion spent and ¥0.5 billion recovered gives −¥2.5 billion, with no other items.

Usage & cautions: Negative is not automatically bad. Distinguish growth investment from maintenance and compare with operating cash flow.

Finance & Accounting

Financing cash flow

Cash movements from borrowing, repayments, share issues, dividends and other financing activities.

Formula / calculation basis
Financing activities section of the cash flow statement

Example: Borrow ¥2 billion, repay ¥1 billion and pay ¥0.3 billion dividends: +¥0.7 billion.

Usage & cautions: Positive may reflect fund raising; negative may reflect repayments or shareholder returns. The sign alone does not indicate quality.

Finance & Accounting

Free cash flow

Cash remaining after investment needs; definitions vary by company and method.

Formula / calculation basis
Common shortcut: operating CF + investing CF; alternative: operating CF − capital expenditure

Example: ¥5 billion operating CF + (−¥3 billion investing CF) = ¥2 billion.

Usage & cautions: Indicates capacity for dividends, debt repayment and growth. Asset sales and M&A can distort the shortcut.

Finance & Accounting

Current ratio

A measure of short-term payment capacity.

Formula / calculation basis
Current assets ÷ current liabilities × 100

Example: ¥20 billion ÷ ¥10 billion × 100 = 200%.

Usage & cautions: Check how readily receivables and inventory can become cash.

Finance & Accounting

Debt-to-equity ratio (D/E)

Interest-bearing debt relative to equity.

Formula / calculation basis
Interest-bearing debt ÷ equity

Example: ¥6 billion ÷ ¥10 billion = 0.6×.

Usage & cautions: Assess debt burdens and industry characteristics.

Finance & Accounting

Interest coverage ratio

How many times business earnings cover interest expense.

Formula / calculation basis
(Operating profit + interest and dividend income) ÷ interest expense

Example: ¥1.2 billion ÷ ¥0.2 billion = 6×.

Usage & cautions: Definitions differ between sources; confirm the calculation basis.

Valuation & Ratios

Dividend payout ratio

The proportion of earnings paid as dividends.

Formula / calculation basis
Annual dividend per share ÷ EPS × 100

Example: ¥40 ÷ ¥100 × 100 = 40%.

Usage & cautions: An unusually high payout warrants a sustainability check.

Valuation & Ratios

Dividend on equity (DOE)

Annual dividends relative to shareholders' equity.

Formula / calculation basis
Total annual dividends ÷ average equity × 100

Example: ¥0.3 billion ÷ ¥10 billion × 100 = 3%.

Usage & cautions: Helps explain dividend policy when earnings fluctuate.

Investing Basics

Income return

Income received while holding an asset, such as dividends or interest.

Usage & cautions: Consider principal price movements as well as yield.

Investing Basics

Capital gain

Profit from selling an asset above its acquisition cost.

Formula / calculation basis
Sale proceeds − acquisition cost − transaction costs

Usage & cautions: Allow for taxes and fees.

Business & Management

Share buyback

A company's purchase of its own outstanding shares.

Usage & cautions: Check whether shares are cancelled, the effect on capital efficiency and available funds.

Business & Management

Management buyout (MBO)

Management acquires its company's shares or business, often with lenders or investment funds. A listed company may seek to go private.

Example: Management teams with a fund and launches a tender offer to acquire public shares.

Usage & cautions: Offer price affects the share price. Ownership, management intentions, business value and financing matter, but prediction is difficult. Offers may fail or change terms. Related: TOB, buybacks and enterprise value.

Stock Market

Tender offer (TOB)

An offer to acquire shares outside regular exchange trading under publicly announced conditions.

Usage & cautions: Check price, completion conditions and withdrawal risk.

Related articlesMBO investing →M&A and restructuring →
Business & Management

Stock options

Rights to acquire shares under specified conditions.

Usage & cautions: Check dilution and exercise terms.

Business & Management

Investor relations (IR)

Company information disclosed to investors.

Usage & cautions: Read earnings releases, securities reports and presentation materials.

Business & Management

Japanese earnings summary (Kessan Tanshin)

A listed company's timely summary of financial results.

Usage & cautions: Review earnings, financial position and outlook, then use the securities report for detail.

Business & Management

Annual report

A report covering a company's annual activities and performance.

Usage & cautions: Useful for understanding strategy, business structure and management policy.

Investing Basics

Kaisha Shikiho

A Japanese company-information publication summarizing businesses and financial performance.

Usage & cautions: Distinguish the publication's forecasts from company guidance.

Stock Market

Market consensus

Market expectations aggregated from analyst forecasts and similar estimates.

Usage & cautions: Differences between actual results and expectations can affect prices.

Stock Market

Earnings surprise

Results that differ substantially from market expectations.

Usage & cautions: Even good results can trigger a price fall if expectations were higher.

Stock Market

Catalyst

An event that can change a stock's valuation, such as a dividend increase, asset sale, ownership change, removal of takeover defenses or listing change.

Usage & cautions: An event does not guarantee a rise. Assess likelihood and what is already priced in.

Stock Market

TOPIX

The Tokyo Stock Price Index, representing broad movements in Japanese equities.

Usage & cautions: Used as a benchmark for Japanese equity funds.

Stock Market

Nikkei 225

A price-weighted index of 225 stocks calculated by Nikkei.

Usage & cautions: Higher-priced constituent shares have greater influence.

Stock Market

Exchange-traded fund (ETF)

A fund listed and traded on an exchange.

Usage & cautions: Check management fees, benchmark and bid–ask spreads.

Stock Market

Initial public offering (IPO)

A company's first public share offering and stock market listing.

Usage & cautions: Read the prospectus and examine supply, demand and lock-up terms.

Stock Market

Cyclical stocks

Shares in businesses whose performance is sensitive to the economic cycle.

Usage & cautions: Consider earnings peaks and troughs across the cycle.

Business & Management

Commodity companies

Companies linked to goods such as oil, metals or grains.

Usage & cautions: Check how commodity prices affect earnings.

Industry & Technology

Semiconductors

Materials with controllable electrical properties and electronic components made from them.

Usage & cautions: Distinguish design, manufacturing, equipment and materials businesses.

Stock Market

Thematic stocks

Stocks attracting attention through a policy, technology or social theme.

Usage & cautions: Separate publicity from actual earnings contributions.

Stock Market

Speculative stocks (shite-kabu)

Japanese term for shares whose prices may move sharply on speculative trading or particular market narratives.

Usage & cautions: Watch for abrupt price changes and reduced liquidity.

Stock Market

Volatility

The magnitude of price fluctuations.

Formula / calculation basis
Standard deviation of returns is a common measure.

Usage & cautions: Used to assess price fluctuation risk.

Stock Market

Oscillators

Technical indicators of market momentum or overbought and oversold conditions.

Usage & cautions: Do not base trades solely on an indicator such as RSI.

Stock Market

Volume-weighted average price (VWAP)

The average execution price weighted by traded volume.

Formula / calculation basis
Σ(execution price × volume) ÷ Σvolume

Example: 100 shares at ¥100 and 300 at ¥110 give ¥107.5.

Usage & cautions: Used to assess intraday execution and compare with market prices.

Stock Market

Time and sales / order book

Time and sales records executed trades; the order book shows quoted prices and quantities.

Usage & cautions: Useful for short-term supply and demand, but beware of misleading orders.

Stock Market

Order types

Instructions such as market, limit and stop orders.

Usage & cautions: Choose between execution priority and price control to suit the purpose.

Stock Market

Daily price limits

Exchange-defined upper and lower limits for a day's price movements.

Usage & cautions: Reaching a limit does not guarantee execution.

Stock Market

Outstanding margin longs and shorts

Unsettled long and short positions in margin trading.

Usage & cautions: A supply-and-demand indicator, not a forecast of future price movements.

Stock Market

Margin long/short ratio

Outstanding margin long positions relative to outstanding margin short positions.

Formula / calculation basis
Outstanding margin longs ÷ outstanding margin shorts

Example: 1 million shares ÷ 0.2 million shares = 5×.

Usage & cautions: Use consistent dates and reporting coverage.

Stock Market

Physical settlement of margin positions

Genbiki pays for a margin long and receives the shares; genwatashi delivers owned shares to settle a margin short.

Usage & cautions: Check settlement fees, interest and stock borrowing charges.

Stock Market

Ex-dividend date

The first trading day on which a buyer is no longer entitled to the relevant dividend or shareholder benefit.

Usage & cautions: Do not confuse it with the last trading day carrying entitlement.

Usage & cautions: Consider effects on real interest rates and purchasing power.

Investing Basics

Intrinsic value

An estimate of a business’s economic worth based on future benefits and risks.

Finance & Accounting

Valuation & Ratios

Dividend yield

Annual dividend per share divided by the share price; not a guaranteed return.

Business & Management

Capital allocation

Management's decisions on using cash for operations, investment, acquisitions or shareholder returns.

Economics & Finance

Economics & Finance

Growth resulting when accumulated returns also earn returns; losses can compound too.

Stock Market
