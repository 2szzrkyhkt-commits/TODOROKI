How to Analyze Japanese Stocks

Japan offers investors a diverse market, from globally competitive manufacturers to domestic service companies. But a low price-to-book ratio alone does not make a stock undervalued. A useful investment thesis starts with the business, its financial strength, and the price paid.

1. Understand the business first

Identify how the company earns revenue, which customers drive demand, and whether its competitive advantages are durable. Consider exposure to overseas sales, commodity prices, and economic cycles. Review the company's annual securities report and investor-relations materials rather than relying on headlines.

2. Evaluate the balance sheet

Check cash, interest-bearing debt, working capital, and the quality of receivables and inventories. The current ratio (current assets divided by current liabilities) can be useful, but its meaning varies by industry. Excess cash may provide flexibility; it is not automatically available for distribution to shareholders.

3. Compare price with earning power

Price-to-earnings (P/E), price-to-book (P/B), return on equity (ROE), and return on assets (ROA) are starting points, not verdicts. A cyclical company's P/E may look unusually low near peak profits. Compare earnings across a full business cycle, assess reinvestment needs, and estimate a range of intrinsic values rather than a single precise number.

4. Inspect cash flow and capital allocation

Compare net income with operating cash flow over several years. Subtract necessary capital expenditures to understand cash available after maintaining the business. Review management's record on acquisitions, dividends, share repurchases, and investment in growth. Share buybacks create value only when the price paid is sensible.

5. Account for yen exposure

A foreign investor's return depends on both the share price in yen and the exchange rate against their home currency. Yen depreciation can reduce home-currency returns even when a stock rises in Tokyo. Exporters and importers may also react differently to currency moves, depending on their cost structures and hedging.

6. Consider governance, taxes and market access

Look at controlling shareholders, cross-shareholdings, board independence, and minority-shareholder protections. Before trading, check whether your broker provides access to Japanese exchanges, the applicable trading units, commissions, custody arrangements, and tax treatment in your jurisdiction. Rules and availability differ by country.

7. Demand a margin of safety

Build conservative scenarios, identify what could go wrong, and compare the estimated value range with the market price. A high-quality company can still be a poor investment at an excessive valuation. No screening rule removes the risk of permanent capital loss.

Research checklist: business model, ten-year profitability, balance-sheet strength, operating cash flow, capital expenditures, governance, valuation, exchange-rate sensitivity, and downside scenarios.
