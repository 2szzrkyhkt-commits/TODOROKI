Interest Rates: How They Affect Stocks, Currency and Bonds

Interest is the price paid for borrowing money and the return received for lending it. Policy rates influence, but do not fully determine, market interest rates.

01. What is an interest rate?

Interest rates are typically expressed as an annual percentage. Central banks set short-term policy rates, while longer-term rates also reflect inflation expectations, growth and risk.

02. When interest rates rise

Higher borrowing costs can reduce consumption and investment. Depositors may receive more interest, although rates differ across products.

03. When interest rates fall

Lower rates can make borrowing cheaper and encourage spending, but they may also signal weak economic conditions.

04. Interest rates and stock valuations

Higher discount rates can lower the present value of future earnings, particularly for companies whose expected profits lie far in the future. Actual share prices also depend on growth and expectations.

05. Interest rates and exchange rates

Higher rates can attract capital into a currency, but exchange rates depend on relative interest rates, inflation, trade flows and market expectations.

06. Interest rates and bond prices

For a conventional fixed-rate bond, market yields and prices generally move in opposite directions. Longer-duration bonds are usually more sensitive.

07. What investors should watch

Look at real interest rates, inflation expectations, debt maturity and whether a rate move was already priced in.

Real versus nominal interest rates

A nominal interest rate is the rate stated on a loan or deposit. The real rate adjusts for inflation. As a rough approximation, real interest rate equals nominal rate minus inflation; the exact relationship also depends on compounding.

For example, a 2% deposit rate alongside 3% inflation implies roughly −1% real purchasing-power growth before tax. A rising nominal rate does not necessarily mean savers are better off if inflation rises faster.

For companies, higher real financing costs can discourage investment and reduce the present value of distant cash flows. But the effect on share prices also depends on earnings expectations, risk premiums and how quickly the economy changes.
