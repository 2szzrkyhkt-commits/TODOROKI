Inflation and the Wealth Gap: Why Financial Resilience Differs

Even households with the same income can experience inflation differently depending on savings, assets and financial flexibility.

01. Why the same income is not the same financial position

When food, energy and daily essentials become more expensive, households whose wages do not keep pace lose spending power. The key difference is whether they have reserves to absorb higher costs.

02. Households with savings and investments

Savings can help cover rising bills and unexpected expenses. Owners of shares or property may sometimes benefit from higher nominal asset values, although prices can also fall.

03. Households with little financial buffer

With little money left after regular expenses, even a modest price rise can force difficult trade-offs. An emergency bill may push the household into debt.

04. Government bonds are not automatically inflation-proof

A fixed-rate bond generally promises specified nominal payments, but inflation can erode their purchasing power. For example, a nominal yield of 2% against inflation of 4% implies an approximate real yield of −2% before tax. Inflation-linked bonds and floating-rate bonds behave differently and carry their own risks.

05. Asset allocation matters

The amount of accessible savings, the type of assets held and debt obligations all affect resilience. Stocks and property are not guaranteed to rise with inflation.

06. What to remember

Having financial assets does not prevent inflation. It may provide greater capacity to absorb it. Compare real returns and liquidity needs rather than assuming any single asset is safe.

Income growth is not the same as wealth growth

Consider two households with the same annual income. One has emergency savings and diversified investments; the other has no buffer and expensive variable-rate debt. Higher prices and interest rates may force the second household to cut spending or borrow more.

Wealth also depends on asset ownership, debt, taxes and time. A rise in the market value of investments is not guaranteed to offset living costs, and asset prices can fall just when cash is needed.

Compare real disposable income, liquid reserves, debt-service costs and the ability to continue saving. These measures give a more useful picture than nominal salary alone.
